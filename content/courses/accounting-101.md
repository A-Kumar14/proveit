# Accounting 101

An introduction to financial accounting. It starts with how transactions are recorded, moves to the three core financial statements, then covers timing rules and basic analysis.

## Recording transactions

### The accounting equation
Every business obeys one equation: assets equal liabilities plus owner's equity. Assets are what the business owns, liabilities are what it owes, and equity is the owners' claim on what is left. Every transaction changes the accounts in a way that keeps both sides of the equation equal.

### Debits and credits
Double-entry bookkeeping records each transaction in at least two accounts. A debit is an entry on the left side and a credit is an entry on the right. Debits increase assets and expenses and decrease liabilities, equity and revenue. Credits do the opposite. In every transaction, total debits equal total credits.

## The core statements

### Income statement
The income statement reports performance over a period. Revenue minus expenses gives net income. It answers whether the business made a profit between two dates, and it ignores cash timing: revenue is counted when earned and expenses when incurred.

### Balance sheet
The balance sheet is a snapshot of assets, liabilities and equity on one date. It must satisfy the accounting equation. Assets are usually listed from most liquid, such as cash, to least liquid, such as buildings, and liabilities are split into current items due within a year and long-term items.

### Cash flow statement
The cash flow statement explains how cash changed over a period. It splits cash into operating activities, investing activities such as buying equipment, and financing activities such as borrowing or paying dividends. A business can show a profit and still run out of cash, which is why this statement is separate.

## Timing and adjustments

### Accrual versus cash accounting
Cash accounting records transactions when money moves. Accrual accounting records revenue when it is earned and expenses when they are incurred, whether or not cash has changed hands. Accrual matches revenue with the costs that produced it, so it gives a truer picture of performance and is required for most larger businesses.

### Depreciation
Depreciation spreads the cost of a long-lived asset over the years it is used. Under straight-line depreciation, cost minus expected salvage value is divided by the useful life in years. The yearly amount is an expense on the income statement, and the asset's book value on the balance sheet falls by the same amount.

## Reading the numbers

### Working capital and liquidity
Working capital is current assets minus current liabilities. The current ratio, current assets divided by current liabilities, shows whether a business can pay its short-term bills. A ratio below 1 means short-term obligations exceed short-term assets, which is a warning sign, though normal levels differ by industry.
