# Chinese 101

An introduction to Mandarin Chinese. It teaches the sound system first, then how written characters are built, then the sentence patterns you need to start a conversation. Pinyin, the Latin spelling system for Mandarin sounds, is used throughout.

## Sounds

### Pinyin initials and finals
Every Mandarin syllable splits into an initial, the starting consonant, and a final, the vowel part. For example, in "ma" the initial is m and the final is a. Some letters sound different from English: q is like "ch" with the tongue forward, x is like "sh" with a smile, and zh is like a hard "j". Learn the sounds with a teacher or recordings, because spelling alone can mislead.

### The four tones
Mandarin has four tones plus a neutral tone, and the tone changes the meaning of a syllable. The first tone is high and flat, the second rises, the third dips and rises, and the fourth falls sharply. The syllable "ma" means mother with the first tone, hemp with the second, horse with the third and scold with the fourth. The neutral tone is short and light.

## Characters

### Radicals and strokes
Chinese characters are built from strokes written in a set order, usually top to bottom and left to right. Many characters contain a radical, a component that hints at meaning or category. For example, the water radical appears in characters related to liquids and rivers. Knowing common radicals helps you look up and remember new characters.

### Simplified and traditional characters
Two written standards exist. Simplified characters have fewer strokes and are used in mainland China and Singapore. Traditional characters are used in Taiwan, Hong Kong and Macau. They describe the same spoken language, and many characters are the same in both. Pick one to learn first, and know the other exists.

## Everyday sentences

### Greetings and introductions
The usual greeting is nǐ hǎo, which means hello. To say who you are, use wǒ jiào followed by your name, meaning "I am called". To ask someone's name, say nǐ jiào shénme míngzi. A polite reply to thanks is bú kèqi, meaning "you're welcome".

### Numbers and measure words
The numbers one to ten are yī, èr, sān, sì, wǔ, liù, qī, bā, jiǔ and shí. Larger numbers are built from these, so eleven is shí yī and twenty is èr shí. When counting nouns, Mandarin needs a measure word between the number and the noun, such as gè for people and general items. Three people is sān gè rén.

### Basic sentence order
Mandarin word order is subject, then verb, then object, like English. Time words come before the verb, so "I eat rice today" is "wǒ jīntiān chī fàn". Verbs do not change form for tense. Words such as le and guo show whether an action is completed or has happened before.

### Asking questions
The simplest question adds the particle ma to the end of a statement. "Nǐ shì xuéshēng" is "you are a student", and "nǐ shì xuéshēng ma" asks whether that is true. Question words such as shénme for what, shéi for who and nǎlǐ for where take the place of the missing information in the sentence and do not move to the front.
